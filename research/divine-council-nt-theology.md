# The Divine Council Motif Fulfilled: Jesus, the Spirit, and Salvation in the New Testament

A research compilation tracing the "divine council" or "heavenly assembly" motif documented in *divine-council-theology.md* (Gen 3:22, Gen 6:1-4, Deut 32:8-9, Psalm 82, 1 Enoch, the Mesopotamian *puḫru*, and the Apkallu) forward into the New Testament and early Christian doctrine. The governing thesis, argued across this literature by Michael Heiser and engaged (in agreement or in tension) by mainstream biblical and patristic scholars, is that the New Testament's Christology, pneumatology, and soteriology are not a rupture from the divine-council picture of the Hebrew Bible but its narrative resolution: the council's presiding deity, its loyal and rebellious members, its imprisoned Watchers, and its disinherited nations all reappear — judged, defeated, or regathered — in the Gospels, Paul, and the early creeds.

---

## 1. Daniel 7:9-14 — The Divine Council's Throne Scene and "One Like a Son of Man"

Daniel 7:9-14 is written in Aramaic and stages the clearest Second Temple bridge between the Hebrew Bible's divine council texts and New Testament Christology. The text (Sefaria, Daniel 7:9, 7:13 — https://www.sefaria.org/Daniel.7.9, https://www.sefaria.org/Daniel.7.13):

**v.9** — חָזֵ֣ה הֲוֵ֗ית עַ֣ד דִּ֤י כׇרְסָוָן֙ רְמִ֔יו וְעַתִּ֥יק יוֹמִ֖ין יְתִ֑ב — "I looked on, as thrones were set in place [*korsavan remiyu*, plural] and the Ancient of Days took His seat. His garment was like white snow, and the hair of His head was like lamb's wool. His throne was tongues of flame; its wheels were blazing fire."

**v.13** — חָזֵ֤ה הֲוֵית֙ בְּחֶזְוֵ֣י לֵֽילְיָ֔א וַאֲרוּ֙ עִם־עֲנָנֵ֣י שְׁמַיָּ֔א כְּבַ֥ר אֱנָ֖שׁ אָתֵ֣ה הֲוָ֑א וְעַד־עַתִּ֤יק יֽוֹמַיָּא֙ מְטָ֔ה — "I looked on, in the night vision, and behold, with the clouds of heaven one like a son of man [*kevar enash*] came, and he reached the Ancient of Days and was presented to Him."

**v.14** adds that "dominion, glory, and kingship were given to him; all peoples and nations of every language must serve him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion that shall not pass away, and his kingship, one that shall not be destroyed."

Michael Heiser's dissertation chapter, "The Divine Council in the Book of Daniel" (condensed and hosted at https://www.thedivinecouncil.com/daniel7.pdf), makes the case that this scene is a direct continuation of the pre-exilic council texts (Deut 4:19-20; 32:8-9) rather than evidence that Second Temple Judaism had abandoned divine plurality. Heiser identifies the passage, following John J. Collins, as belonging to "the tradition of biblical throne visions," and "the '[widespread] idea of a heavenly court and council of divine beings'" (Heiser, "The Divine Council in the Book of Daniel," p.1, quoting John J. Collins, *Daniel: A Commentary on the Book of Daniel*, Hermeneia [Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 1993], 300, 303). Heiser argues that the "plurality of thrones... is evident in Daniel 7:9," and that the text "clearly states that it was the council that was collectively seated (בְּתִיו דִּי) along with the Ancient of Days" — rejecting readings (both the older critical view of James Montgomery and the rabbinic tradition of Rabbi Akiba, who read the second throne as reserved for David) that would eliminate the plurality (Heiser, "Daniel in the Book of Daniel," pp.1-2, citing Collins, *Daniel*, 301 n.210).

Heiser's central argument is that the "one like a human being" of v.13 functions as a **second, distinct divine figure** — Yahweh-El's vice-regent — rather than a mere symbol for Israel or an identification with the archangel Michael. He builds this on the epithet "rider of the clouds" (עִם־עֲנָנֵי שְׁמַיָּא, Dan 7:13), elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible applied only to Yahweh himself (Ps 68:5; Isa 19:1; Deut 33:26), citing J.A. Emerton's judgment that "the act of coming with the clouds suggests a theophany of Yahweh himself. If Daniel vii.13 does not refer to a divine being, then it is the only exception out of about seventy passages in the Old Testament" (Emerton, "The Origin of the Son of Man Imagery," *JTS* 9.2 [1958]: 231-232, quoted in Heiser, "Daniel," p.9-10). Heiser explicitly rejects the identification of the "one like a human being" with Michael, noting that Daniel itself distinguishes Michael — "one of the chief princes" (Dan 10:13) — from the singular "Prince of the host" / "Prince of princes" (Dan 8:11, 25) who outranks him: "Michael cannot simultaneously be one among equals in this class of beings and be above them" (Heiser, "Daniel," pp.16-17, engaging Collins, *Daniel*, 309-310, 318-319). Heiser also notes that Collins himself identifies the "holy ones of the Most High" who share the son of man's dominion (Dan 7:18, 27) as "in all probability heavenly beings" (Collins, *Daniel*, 313-317, cited in Heiser, "Daniel," p.20) — i.e., the second-tier *bene elohim* of Deut 32:8-9, over whom the son-of-man figure is granted authority.

Heiser closes the chapter by drawing the explicit New Testament line: "Jesus quotes Daniel 7:13 (cf. Matt. 26) when pressed by Caiaphas the high priest to confess who he was — HE is the rider on the clouds, the 'son of man,' the 'one like a human being' who is Yahweh's co-ruler" (Heiser, "Daniel," p.21). Alan Segal's *Two Powers in Heaven: Early Rabbinic Reports about Christianity and Gnosticism* (Leiden: Brill, 1977) independently treats Daniel 7:9ff. as one of the primary biblical texts later rabbis had to defend against the "two powers" heresy — the belief, which Segal traces into the first century, that a "principal angelic or hypostatic manifestation in heaven was equivalent to God" (Segal, discussed further in §3 below).

## 2. Psalm 82 in John 10:34-36 — Jesus's Own Citation of the Council

John 10:34-36, in the Greek text (Nestle 1904, via BibleHub interlinear — https://biblehub.com/text/john/10-34.htm and adjacent pages):

**v.34** — ἀπεκρίθη αὐτοῖς ὁ Ἰησοῦς Οὐκ ἔστιν γεγραμμένον ἐν τῷ νόμῳ ὑμῶν ὅτι Ἐγὼ εἶπα Θεοί ἐστε; — "Jesus answered them, 'Is it not written in your Law, "I said, you are gods"?'"

**v.35** — εἰ ἐκείνους εἶπεν θεοὺς πρὸς οὓς ὁ λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ οὐ δύναται λυθῆναι ἡ γραφή — "If he called them gods to whom the word of God came — and Scripture cannot be broken —"

**v.36** — ὃν ὁ Πατὴρ ἡγίασεν καὶ ἀπέστειλεν εἰς τὸν κόσμον ὑμεῖς λέγετε ὅτι Βλασφημεῖς, ὅτι εἶπον Υἱὸς τοῦ Θεοῦ εἰμι; — "do you say of him whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world, 'You are blaspheming,' because I said, 'I am the Son of God'?"

The citation is to Psalm 82:6, the same verse establishing the divine council's *elohim* in §4 of the benchmark file. Two scholarly readings coexist. The **mainstream evangelical reading**, represented in commentaries by D.A. Carson (*The Gospel According to John* [Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1991]) and treated at length by Craig Keener (*The Gospel of John: A Commentary*, 2 vols. [Peabody, MA: Hendrickson, 2003]), takes Psalm 82's "gods" as human recipients of God's law or judicial office — Keener specifically notes the second-century rabbinic application of the verse to Israel gathered at Sinai as recipients of Torah — and reads John 10:34-36 as a Jewish *qal va-ḥomer* ("how much more," or *a fortiori*) argument: if mere human officeholders could be called *elohim* on the strength of a divine commission, then how much more fittingly does the title "Son of God" belong to the one "whom the Father consecrated and sent into the world." On this reading, Jesus is not claiming membership in a council of divine beings but exploiting a scriptural precedent to defend an infinitely greater, unique claim to deity.

Michael Heiser's ETS 2010 paper (already cited in the benchmark file, §4.2 — "Should the Plural אלהים of Psalm 82 Be Understood as Men or Divine Beings?," https://thedivinecouncil.com/ETS2010Psalm82.pdf) argues instead that this reading fails on Heiser's own philological case for Psalm 82's *elohim*: since no Hebrew Bible text anywhere places "human judges" in a heavenly council in the clouds (Ps 89:5-7), Jesus's appeal to Ps 82:6 is best read as **an argument from the lesser divine beings to the greater** — Jesus asserting membership among, and explicit superiority to, the very "sons of the Most High" (*bene elyon*) of the council whom Yahweh judges in that psalm. On Heiser's reading, the force of Jesus's argument is heightened, not diminished, by the divine-council referent: if Yahweh himself could apply the term *elohim* to lesser members of his own heavenly court without blasphemy, his hearers have no ground to charge blasphemy against the one whom the Father "consecrated and sent" — a designation of a wholly different order of commissioning than that given to any *bene elohim*. Both readings agree that John 10:34-36 is Jesus asserting unique, superior status relative to Psalm 82's council of *elohim*, not merely comparing himself to ordinary human beings; they diverge only on whether Ps 82's original referents are themselves human or the same non-human council members catalogued throughout the benchmark file's §4.

## 3. The Angel of YHWH — the Second Temple "Second Power" Background

Exodus 23:20-23 supplies the Hebrew Bible's clearest precedent for a distinct-yet-divine agent who bears God's own name and authority. The Hebrew text (Sefaria, Exodus 23:20-21 — https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.23.20-23):

**v.20** — הִנֵּ֨ה אָנֹכִ֜י שֹׁלֵ֤חַ מַלְאָךְ֙ לְפָנֶ֔יךָ לִשְׁמָרְךָ֖ בַּדָּ֑רֶךְ וְלַהֲבִ֣יאֲךָ֔ אֶל־הַמָּק֖וֹם אֲשֶׁ֥ר הֲכִנֹֽתִי׃ — "I am sending an angel [*mal'akh*] before you to guard you on the way and to bring you to the place that I have made ready."

**v.21** — הִשָּׁ֧מֶר מִפָּנָ֛יו וּשְׁמַ֥ע בְּקֹל֖וֹ אַל־תַּמֵּ֣ר בּ֑וֹ כִּ֣י לֹ֤א יִשָּׂא֙ לְפִשְׁעֲכֶ֔ם כִּ֥י שְׁמִ֖י בְּקִרְבּֽוֹ׃ — "Pay heed to him and obey him. Do not defy him, for he will not pardon your offenses, since My Name is in him."

This angel — who bears the divine Name, whose obedience is owed as if to God, and who is said to be capable of *not* pardoning transgression (implying the capacity to pardon it) — became the central proof-text for what Alan Segal calls the "Two Powers in Heaven" heresy debated inside and outside rabbinic Judaism. Segal's monograph documents the *Babylonian Talmud*, *Sanhedrin* 38b, where a "Min" (heretic) argues from Exodus 24:1 ("Come up to YHWH") combined with Exodus 23:21 ("My name is in him") that a second, angelic bearer of the divine name — identified in the sugya as Metatron — must be a second deity alongside the Father:

> "It was Metatron... whose name is similar to that of his Master, for it is written, *For My name is in Him* (Ex. 23:21). 'But if so, we should worship him!' 'The same passage however,' replied R. Idi, 'says: Be not rebellious against Him.' 'But if so, why is it stated: *He will not pardon your transgression*?' (Ex. 23:21)."
(*b. Sanhedrin* 38b, trans. Epstein, quoted in Alan F. Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven: Early Rabbinic Reports about Christianity and Gnosticism* [Leiden: Brill, 1977], 68-69)

Segal's own analysis states directly: "Obviously this is another case of heretics believing in a principal angel with divine perquisites because the Lord's name is in him.... Now we see how the name of YHWH was associated with the mediator" (Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven*, 68-69). Segal further connects this precisely to first-century Christian exegesis: "If we take the literature in the New Testament as characteristic of some kinds of heresy in the first century, we find that this is not an entirely inaccurate description of Christian exegesis. Ex. 23:20 is most often taken to refer to John the Baptist (Mk. 1:2, Mt. 11:10)... Furthermore, one of the things which, according to the New Testament, most upsets the Jews about Jesus is precisely that he does claim the power to forgive sins (Mk. 2:7 f.)" (Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven*, 70). Segal's conclusion: the rabbis "accused heretics... not merely with the charge of believing in angels... but with the charge of believing that a certain principal angel was a special mediator between God and man," a mediation that "might have involved the forgiving of sins" or "the belief that the angel participated in God's divinity by appropriating one of His names," and that "some varieties of the heresy go back to the first century" (Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven*, 70-71).

The same figure recurs in the patriarchal theophanies: in Genesis 18:1-2, "Yahweh appeared" to Abraham in the form of three visitors, one of whom is repeatedly addressed in the narrative as "the LORD" (יְהֹוָה, Gen 18:13, 17, 20, 22) even while remaining distinguishable from the two "angels" who depart for Sodom in chapter 19; and in Exodus 33:2-3, 14-15, Yahweh offers to send "an angel" ahead of Israel in his own stead, but Moses refuses to proceed without Yahweh's own "Presence" (פָּנַי) going with them — implying the angel-substitute and Yahweh's own presence are related but distinguishable categories of divine agency. Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm: Recovering the Supernatural Worldview of the Bible* (Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press, 2015) devotes extended treatment to the Angel of Yahweh as the primary Old Testament conceptual bridge to New Testament binitarian language about the Father and the Son — the same figure Segal traces into the Metatron and "second power" traditions that early rabbis worked to suppress once Christian theology built on it explicitly.

## 4. Philippians 2:6-11 — Kenosis and the Cosmic Hierarchy

The Christ-hymn of Philippians 2:6-11 is widely regarded as one of the earliest pieces of Christian liturgical material embedded in the New Testament. The Greek text (Nestle 1904, via BibleHub — https://biblehub.com/text/philippians/2-6.htm, 2-7, 2-8, 2-9, 2-10):

**v.6** — ὃς ἐν μορφῇ Θεοῦ ὑπάρχων οὐχ ἁρπαγμὸν ἡγήσατο τὸ εἶναι ἴσα Θεῷ — "who, though he was in the form of God, did not regard equality with God as something to be grasped [ἁρπαγμὸν]."

**v.7** — ἀλλὰ ἑαυτὸν ἐκένωσεν μορφὴν δούλου λαβών, ἐν ὁμοιώματι ἀνθρώπων γενόμενος· καὶ σχήματι εὑρεθεὶς ὡς ἄνθρωπος — "but emptied himself [ἐκένωσεν, the root of the traditional theological term *kenosis*], taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form..."

**v.8** — ἐταπείνωσεν ἑαυτὸν γενόμενος ὑπήκοος μέχρι θανάτου, θανάτου δὲ σταυροῦ — "he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross."

**v.9-10** — διὸ καὶ ὁ θεὸς αὐτὸν ὑπερύψωσεν... ἵνα ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι Ἰησοῦ πᾶν γόνυ κάμψῃ ἐπουρανίων καὶ ἐπιγείων καὶ καταχθονίων — "Therefore God has highly exalted him... so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven [ἐπουρανίων] and on earth [ἐπιγείων] and under the earth [καταχθονίων]."

The tripartite triad ἐπουρανίων καὶ ἐπιγείων καὶ καταχθονίων has no exact parallel elsewhere in Paul, and its scope has been debated at length. A 2025 *New Testament Studies* article by Magnus Rabel surveys the field and confirms that the *majority* scholarly position takes the triad as an "inclusive" formula encompassing "heaven, earth, and hell" (German *Himmel, Erde und Hölle*) "and their respective inhabitants: angels, humans and demons" — a reading defended by, among others, P.T. O'Brien, *The Epistle to the Philippians: A Commentary on the Greek Text* (NIGTC; Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1991), 244, and Ralph P. Martin, *Carmen Christi*, 2nd ed. (SNTSMS 4; Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1983), 259, tracing back to Ernst Lohmeyer, *Kyrios* (Heidelberg, 1928), 59-60 (Magnus Rabel, "The Entire Cosmos' Voluntary and Involuntary Homage to Jesus as Lord: An Investigation into the Scope and Background of Philippians 2.9-11 in Psalm 148 and Isaiah 45.20-5," *New Testament Studies* 71 [2025]: 64-79, at 67-68, DOI: 10.1017/S0028688524000225). Rabel's own contribution argues for a minority alternative — that the triad denotes the totality of creation without necessarily including hostile spirits — but he documents that the dominant reading in Pauline scholarship for a century has read the third term, καταχθονίων ("under the earth"), as the same subterranean domain that houses hostile or imprisoned spiritual powers, directly continuous with the tiered cosmology already established in the benchmark file's Enoch material (§5-6): a heavenly tier of loyal and rebellious *elohim*, an earthly tier of humanity, and a subterranean tier of bound or judged spirits (1 Enoch 10's Watchers cast "into the darkness... under rocks... until the day of great judgment"). On this widely-held reading, Philippians 2:10-11 stages exactly the scene the benchmark file's Mesopotamian and Enochic material anticipates: the assembly of *every* tier of the cosmos — celestial, terrestrial, and infernal — bowing to Christ's exaltation in a single, comprehensive cosmic acclamation.

## 5. Colossians 1:15-20 and 2:15 — Cosmic Reconciliation and Disarming the Powers

Colossians 1:15-17 identifies Christ as the agent through whom the entire populated cosmos — including the divine council's own hierarchy — was created. The Greek text (Nestle 1904, via BibleHub — https://biblehub.com/text/colossians/1-15.htm, 1-16):

**v.15** — ὅς ἐστιν εἰκὼν τοῦ Θεοῦ τοῦ ἀοράτου, πρωτότοκος πάσης κτίσεως — "He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation."

**v.16** — ὅτι ἐν αὐτῷ ἐκτίσθη τὰ πάντα ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς καὶ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, τὰ ὁρατὰ καὶ τὰ ἀόρατα, εἴτε θρόνοι εἴτε κυριότητες εἴτε ἀρχαὶ εἴτε ἐξουσίαι· τὰ πάντα δι' αὐτοῦ καὶ εἰς αὐτὸν ἔκτισται — "for by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether **thrones** [θρόνοι] or **dominions** [κυριότητες] or **rulers** [ἀρχαὶ] or **powers** [ἐξουσίαι] — all things were created through him and for him."

This catalogue of titled ranks — the same bureaucratic vocabulary Daniel 7-10 and Deuteronomy 32:8-9 use for the tiered *bene elohim* of the divine council — is, per Colossians, both created by and subordinate to Christ. Colossians 2:15 then narrates their defeat:

> ἀπεκδυσάμενος τὰς ἀρχὰς καὶ τὰς ἐξουσίας ἐδειγμάτισεν ἐν παρρησίᾳ, θριαμβεύσας αὐτοὺς ἐν αὐτῷ — "He disarmed [ἀπεκδυσάμενος] the rulers [ἀρχὰς] and authorities [ἐξουσίας] and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them" (Col 2:15, BibleHub interlinear — https://biblehub.com/text/colossians/2-15.htm).

Walter Wink's foundational study, *Naming the Powers: The Language of Power in the New Testament* (Philadelphia: Fortress Press, 1984), treats Colossians 1:16 and 2:15 as central texts in cataloguing the New Testament's full vocabulary of "power" language — θρόνοι, κυριότητες, ἀρχαί, ἐξουσίαι, along with δυνάμεις ("powers/miracles") and κοσμοκράτορες ("world-rulers," Eph 6:12) — devoting a dedicated analytical category to "fallen angels, evil spirits, demons" within that taxonomy and treating Colossians 2:13-15 directly as one of the disputed texts describing Christ's triumph over this hierarchy. Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* situates this same material as the New Testament's confrontation with precisely the rebellious, corrupted *elohim* of Psalm 82 — the "gods" whom Yahweh judges and sentences to "die like mortals" for their misrule over the nations (Ps 82:6-7) — arguing that Paul's "principalities and powers" language in Colossians and Ephesians 6 directly continues the divine-council vocabulary of Deuteronomy 32:8-9 and Psalm 82, now applied to their decisive judgment in Christ. On this reading, Colossians 2:15's "disarming" of the ἀρχαὶ καὶ ἐξουσίαι is the New Testament's counterpart to 1 Enoch 10's binding of Azazel and Semjaza — the same class of rebellious divine beings, publicly humiliated and stripped of the authority Deuteronomy 32:8-9 had originally allotted to them over the nations.

## 6. 1 Peter 3:18-20 — Proclamation to "the Spirits in Prison"

1 Peter 3:18-20 is among the most exegetically contested passages in the New Testament. The Greek text (Nestle 1904, via BibleHub — https://biblehub.com/text/1_peter/3-18.htm, 3-19, 3-20):

**v.18** — ὅτι καὶ Χριστὸς ἅπαξ περὶ ἁμαρτιῶν ἀπέθανεν, δίκαιος ὑπὲρ ἀδίκων, ἵνα ὑμᾶς προσαγάγῃ τῷ Θεῷ, θανατωθεὶς μὲν σαρκὶ ζωοποιηθεὶς δὲ πνεύματι — "For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit."

**v.19** — ἐν ᾧ καὶ τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ πνεύμασιν πορευθεὶς ἐκήρυξεν — "in which he went and proclaimed [ἐκήρυξεν] to the spirits [πνεύμασιν] in prison [ἐν φυλακῇ]."

**v.20** — ἀπειθήσασίν ποτε ὅτε ἀπεξεδέχετο ἡ τοῦ Θεοῦ μακροθυμία ἐν ἡμέραις Νῶε κατασκευαζομένης κιβωτοῦ, εἰς ἣν ὀλίγοι, τοῦτ' ἔστιν ὀκτὼ ψυχαί, διεσώθησαν δι' ὕδατος — "who formerly did not obey, when God's patience waited in the days of Noah, while the ark was being built, in which a few, that is, eight persons, were brought safely through water."

The standard technical monograph on this text is William Joseph Dalton, S.J., *Christ's Proclamation to the Spirits: A Study of 1 Peter 3:18-4:6*, 2nd, fully revised ed., Analecta Biblica 23 (Rome: Pontifical Biblical Institute, 1989; 1st ed. 1965). Dalton's central argument, widely accepted in subsequent scholarship, is that the "spirits [πνεύμασιν, not the ψυχαί — "souls" — used elsewhere of deceased humans] in prison" are not the disembodied souls of the human generation drowned in the Flood, but the same rebellious angelic Watchers of 1 Enoch 6-16 and Genesis 6:1-4, whom 1 Enoch 10:4, 12 describes as bound "in the desert... under rocks" "until the day of great judgment." On this reading — noted already by patristic writers and revived as the majority critical position by Dalton's monograph — 1 Peter 3:19-20 depicts the risen Christ, "made alive in the spirit" (v.18), making a proclamation of victory (κηρύσσω, the same verb used of gospel proclamation elsewhere in the NT, though here directed at judgment rather than invitation) to the very Watchers whose binding and imprisonment the benchmark file documents in 1 Enoch 10:4-16 (§5). Michael Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* treats this connection as a linchpin of his New Testament argument, reading 1 Peter 3:18-20 as Christ's direct confrontation of the same imprisoned Watchers first bound by the archangels Michael and Raphael in 1 Enoch — the risen Christ, having "died... to bring us to God," extending his triumph over sin and death into a proclamation of victory to the very beings whose primordial rebellion (Gen 6:1-4; 1 Enoch 6-11) the entire benchmark file traces.

## 7. Acts 2 — Pentecost as the Reversal of Babel and Deuteronomy 32

The benchmark file's §3 establishes Deuteronomy 32:8-9's restored Qumran/LXX reading: Elyon "fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God," disinheriting the nations of the earth to the rule of the *bene elohim* while retaining Jacob/Israel as his own portion. Acts 2 stages the New Testament's narrative reversal of that disinheritance. Luke's list of the nations present at Pentecost (Acts 2:9-11) draws, according to Craig Keener's *Acts: An Exegetical Commentary* (4 vols.; Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2012-2015) and his popular treatment "Pentecost as a Reversal of Babel" (https://craigkeener.com/pentecost-as-a-reversal-of-babel/), directly on the Table of Nations tradition of Genesis 10: "the table of nations in Genesis 10 informs Luke's list of nations in Acts 2:9-11 (as Gen 10 informed most Jewish lists of nations)," making "an allusion to Babel in Gen 11:1-9 in the same context" likely, since Babel was "the only scattering of languages in the OT and hence the only *potential* background for Luke's story shared by all his ideal audience." Keener frames the reversal as a structural inversion: "At Babel: God descended and scattered tongues to prevent unity... At Pentecost: the Spirit descends and scatters tongues to create multicultural unity."

Michael Heiser makes the same connection explicit with respect to Deuteronomy 32:8-9 specifically, in material drawn from *The Unseen Realm* (Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press, 2015), 112-115, 120, 296-306: "The famous story of the building of the Tower of Babel is about much more than ill-fated construction" (Heiser, *Unseen Realm*, 112) — it is the narrative account of the disinheritance later described theologically in Deut 32:8-9. Heiser argues that the Greek term used for the "confusion" of Acts 2 deliberately echoes the LXX vocabulary of the Babel narrative, and states the thesis directly: "Babel's disinheritance was going to be rectified by the message of Jesus and His Spirit" (Heiser, *Unseen Realm*, 299). On this reading, the Pentecost list of nations (Acts 2:9-11) represents a deliberate, representative sampling of the nations disinherited at Babel and allotted to the *bene elohim* in Deut 32:8 — now being regathered, in their own native tongues, under the direct address of Yahweh's own Spirit rather than through the intermediary rule of the elohim to whom they were assigned.

This reading has patristic precedent. Gregory of Nazianzus's *Oration 41* ("On Pentecost"), §§15-16, makes the reversal explicit: contrasting the "confusion of tongues" at Babel — where, in his words, "God, having ruined their shared knowledge by dividing their language, thus foiled their attempt" — with Pentecost, where "the present miracle... flows from one spirit, is poured out to many, and unites us together once more" (Gregory of Nazianzus, *Oration* 41.16, trans. per Alex Poulos, "Gregory's Oration on Pentecost: A Translation from 41.15-16," https://alexpoulos.com/2012/09/28/gregorys-oration-on-pentecost-a-translation-from-41-15-6/; standard critical text in *Sources Chrétiennes* 358, and English in *NPNF*, 2nd series, vol. 7). Beverly Roberts Gaventa's *Acts* commentary (Abingdon New Testament Commentaries; Nashville: Abingdon Press, 2003) likewise situates the Pentecost narrative within Luke's programmatic concern for the ingathering of "all nations" as the theological center of Acts' opening chapters, a concern the Babel-reversal reading renders as the direct undoing of Deuteronomy 32:8-9's original division.

## 8. The Holy Spirit — From Genesis 1:2 to Trinitarian Formulation

The Spirit's agency in the created order is attested from the very first verses of the Hebrew Bible. Genesis 1:2 (Sefaria, Genesis 1:2 — https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.1.2):

> וְהָאָ֗רֶץ הָיְתָ֥ה תֹ֙הוּ֙ וָבֹ֔הוּ וְחֹ֖שֶׁךְ עַל־פְּנֵ֣י תְה֑וֹם וְר֣וּחַ אֱלֹהִ֔ים מְרַחֶ֖פֶת עַל־פְּנֵ֥י הַמָּֽיִם — "the earth being unformed and void, with darkness over the surface of the deep, and the wind/spirit of God [*ruach elohim*] hovering over the water."

This *ruach elohim* is not a peripheral figure in the Hebrew Bible's own theology: Isaiah 63:10 identifies the divine Spirit as a personal agent capable of being grieved by covenant rebellion — וְהֵ֛מָּה מָר֥וּ וְעִצְּב֖וּ אֶת־ר֣וּחַ קׇדְשׁ֑וֹ וַיֵּהָפֵ֥ךְ לָהֶ֛ם לְאוֹיֵ֖ב הוּא נִלְחַם־בָּֽם — "But they rebelled and grieved His holy spirit [*ruach qodsho*]; so He turned into their enemy, and Himself made war against them" (Sefaria, Isaiah 63:10 — https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.63.10), while Ezekiel 37:1-14's vision of the dry bones stages the *ruach* as the specific agent of Israel's national resurrection, given "from the four winds" to reanimate the slain. This same *ruach*, already personal and active in creation, judgment, grief, and resurrection in the Hebrew Bible, is presented in the Gospels and Acts as the agent of Christ's own conception (Luke 1:35), empowerment (Luke 4:14, 18), and the church's Pentecostal ingathering documented in §7 above.

The doctrinal question of the Spirit's own status within the divine identity was not resolved until the fourth century. The Creed adopted at the first ecumenical council, Nicaea (325 CE), addressed the Son's relation to the Father at length but on the Spirit said only "καὶ εἰς τὸ Ἅγιον Πνεῦμα" — "and in the Holy Spirit" — before proceeding directly to its anathemas, leaving the Spirit's precise ontological status unaddressed. The second ecumenical council, Constantinople (381 CE), responding directly to the Pneumatomachian ("Spirit-fighting") or Macedonian controversy that denied the Spirit's full divinity, expanded this clause substantially. The Greek text of the resulting Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed's pneumatological article reads:

> Καὶ εἰς τὸ Πνεῦμα τὸ Ἅγιον, τὸ κύριον, τὸ ζωοποιόν, τὸ ἐκ τοῦ Πατρὸς ἐκπορευόμενον, τὸ σὺν Πατρὶ καὶ Υἱῷ συμπροσκυνούμενον καὶ συνδοξαζόμενον, τὸ λαλῆσαν διὰ τῶν προφητῶν — "And in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Giver of Life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified, who spoke through the prophets" (standard critical Greek text, e.g. Denzinger, *Enchiridion Symbolorum*, and Philip Schaff, *The Creeds of Christendom*, vol. 2).

The council's ascription to the Spirit of the divine titles κύριον ("Lord") and ζωοποιόν ("Life-giver," echoing Gen 2:7 and Ezek 37) and of full liturgical co-worship (συμπροσκυνούμενον) with the Father and Son settled, at the level of official creedal formulation, the question implicit throughout the Hebrew Bible's *ruach elohim* material: whether the divine Spirit active in creation, prophecy, and resurrection is himself fully God rather than merely a created instrument or impersonal force. Lewis Ayres's standard modern study, *Nicaea and Its Legacy: An Approach to Fourth-Century Trinitarian Theology* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), traces this fourth-century pro-Nicene consensus-building in detail, situating the 381 pneumatological expansion as the culmination of decades of theological argument (associated especially with Athanasius's *Letters to Serapion* and the Cappadocian fathers, including Gregory of Nazianzus, whose Pentecost oration is cited in §7 above) establishing the Spirit's full consubstantiality with the Father and Son — the final term of the Trinitarian formula whose conceptual roots this section traces to Genesis 1:2's *ruach elohim*.

## Synthesis: The New Testament as Resolution

Read together against the benchmark file's Old Testament and Second Temple material, these eight bodies of New Testament and patristic evidence do not introduce a new cosmology; they resolve the one already documented. Each element of the earlier divine-council narrative recurs here in a state of completion, defeat, or reversal.

**1. Jesus is enthroned above and superior to the council, not merely admitted into it.** Daniel 7:9-14's "one like a son of man," received before the plural thrones of the assembled council and granted "an everlasting dominion that shall not pass away," is directly claimed by Jesus (Matt 26:64, citing Dan 7:13) and is read by Heiser as Yahweh-El's own vice-regent, distinct from but co-equal in the divine identity with the Ancient of Days — the same conceptual position occupied, in Philippians 2:9-11, by the one to whom "every knee" bows "in heaven and on earth and under the earth," and in Colossians 1:15-17, by the one through whom the entire ranked hierarchy of θρόνοι, κυριότητες, ἀρχαί, and ἐξουσίαι — the same *bene elohim* bureaucracy of Deuteronomy 32:8-9 and Psalm 82 — was itself created. Jesus's citation of Psalm 82:6 in John 10:34-36 is best read, on Heiser's argument, as the same claim in miniature: membership in, and explicit superiority to, the council whose corrupt members that psalm judges.

**2. The rebellious and corrupt elohim are disarmed, and the imprisoned Watchers are directly confronted.** Psalm 82's *elohim*, judged and stripped of immortality "for their corrupt administration" of the nations (benchmark file §4), reappear as the ἀρχαὶ καὶ ἐξουσίαι ("rulers and authorities") whom Christ, per Colossians 2:15, "disarmed... and made a public spectacle of," precisely echoing 1 Enoch 10's public binding and future judgment of Azazel and Semjaza (benchmark file §5). 1 Peter 3:18-20's proclamation to "the spirits in prison" — on Dalton's and Heiser's shared reading — is the New Testament's narration of the risen Christ making direct contact with those same bound Watchers, extending his resurrection triumph into the very location (1 Enoch 10:4, "under rocks... in the desert") where the benchmark file's Book of Watchers material left them imprisoned pending "the day of great judgment."

**3. The nations divided at Babel and allotted to the elohim are regathered.** Deuteronomy 32:8-9's restored "sons of God" reading — Elyon's apportionment of the nations among the *bene elohim*, with Jacob/Israel reserved as Yahweh's own direct portion (benchmark file §3) — finds its narrative reversal in Acts 2, where the Spirit's descent at Pentecost addresses representative members of exactly those divided nations, in their own tongues, without mediation through the *bene elohim* to whom they were allotted at Babel. Both Craig Keener's and Michael Heiser's independent treatments of this text read the list of nations in Acts 2:9-11 as a deliberate echo of the Table of Nations (Gen 10) that Babel (Gen 11) originally scattered, a reading with roots already in Gregory of Nazianzus's fourth-century Pentecost homiletics.

**4. The plural-yet-one pattern of Genesis 1:26 and 3:22 is resolved, not erased, in Trinitarian doctrine.** The benchmark file's opening section (§1) documents the Hebrew Bible's earliest plural-address texts — "let us make man," "like one of us" — as addresses by Yahweh to the members of his heavenly court, a linguistic phenomenon distinct from, and historically prior to, later Trinitarian theology. The New Testament and patristic material surveyed here shows how the *conceptual* problem those texts raise — a Yahweh who is both singular in supremacy and, in some sense, plural in his own self-reference and in his relationship to a "second power" bearing his own Name (§3 above; Exod 23:20-23) — is ultimately resolved not by a return to the populated, ontologically heterogeneous council of Psalm 82, but by the confession, formalized at Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together constitute a single, uncreated divine identity distinct in kind from every created *elohim* of the council — Jesus and the Spirit sharing fully in that uncreated identity in a way no member of the *bene elohim*, however exalted, ever did. The "second power" speculation Alan Segal traces from Exodus 23:20-23 through Second Temple Judaism into the earliest Christian exegesis of Jesus (§3) is, on this account, neither the rabbis' heresy nor a lapse into the old Canaanite pantheon, but the historical seedbed out of which Trinitarian doctrine — Yahweh's ancient, singular supremacy over a real but subordinate heavenly host, now understood to include a Son and a Spirit who are not subordinate members of that host but co-equal with the Father — was eventually and deliberately articulated.
