# The Divine Council Motif in the Ancient Near East and Biblical/Second Temple Literature

A research compilation of primary-source evidence and scholarship on the "divine council" or "heavenly assembly" motif, tracing its presence in the Hebrew Bible, Second Temple Jewish literature (1 Enoch, Book of Giants), and its Mesopotamian antecedents.

---

## 1. Genesis 3 — The Serpent, the Tree of Knowledge, and "Like One of Us" (Gen 3:22)

The Eden narrative culminates in Yahweh's statement after the man and woman eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. The Hebrew text of Genesis 3:22 reads:

> וַיֹּ֣אמֶר יְהֹוָ֗ה אֱלֹהִ֗ים הֵ֤ן הָֽאָדָם֙ הָיָה֙ כְּאַחַ֣ד מִמֶּ֔נּוּ לָדַ֖עַת ט֣וֹב וָרָ֑ע

Rendered by the JPS Tanakh (Gender-Sensitive Edition): "Now that humankind has become like any of us, knowing good and bad, what if one should stretch out a hand and take also from the tree of life and eat, and live forever!" (Sefaria, Hebrew/English Tanakh, Genesis 3:22 — https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.3.22).

The phrase כְּאַחַ֣ד מִמֶּ֔נּוּ ("like one of us") uses first-person plural, paralleling the "let us make man" of Genesis 1:26. Medieval Jewish exegesis already wrestled with the plural: Rashi's commentary on Genesis 3:22 glosses "IS BECOME LIKE ONE OF US" as referring to "the Being who is One, Unique amongst us," i.e., attempting to resolve the plural in a monotheistic direction — evidence that the plural form was recognized as exegetically live already in the classical rabbinic period (Sefaria, Rashi on Genesis 3:22 — https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.3.22).

Modern divine-council scholarship (following the same interpretive logic later applied systematically to Gen 1:26, 11:7, and Isa 6:8) reads "one of us" as Yahweh addressing the members of his heavenly court — the same class of being invoked in Job 1:6 and Psalm 82 — rather than a plural of majesty or an address to the Trinity (a later theological reading anachronistic to the text). Michael Heiser's broader argument (see §3 and §4 below) is that this plural-address pattern in Genesis 1, 3, and 11 is the earliest textual layer establishing Yahweh as head of a council of "elohim" beings, a premise on which the later Deuteronomy 32 and Psalm 82 texts build explicitly.

## 2. Genesis 6:1-4 — The "Sons of God," the Daughters of Men, and the Nephilim

Genesis 6:1-4 narrates a brief but consequential episode immediately preceding the flood account. The Hebrew and JPS (Gender-Sensitive Edition) English text (Sefaria, Genesis 6:1-4 — https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.6.1-4):

**v.1** — וַֽיְהִי֙ כִּֽי־הֵחֵ֣ל הָֽאָדָ֔ם לָרֹ֖ב עַל־פְּנֵ֣י הָֽאֲדָמָ֑ה וּבָנ֖וֹת יֻלְּד֥וּ לָהֶֽם — "When humankind began to increase on earth and daughters were born to them,"

**v.2** — וַיִּרְא֤וּ בְנֵי־הָֽאֱלֹהִים֙ אֶת־בְּנ֣וֹת הָֽאָדָ֔ם כִּ֥י טֹבֹ֖ת הֵ֑נָּה וַיִּקְח֤וּ לָהֶם֙ נָשִׁ֔ים מִכֹּ֖ל אֲשֶׁ֥ר בָּחָֽרוּ — "the divine beings [bene ha'elohim] saw how pleasing the human women were and took wives from among those who delighted them."

**v.3** — God's response, limiting human days to 120 years.

**v.4** — הַנְּפִלִ֞ים הָי֣וּ בָאָ֘רֶץ֮ בַּיָּמִ֣ים הָהֵם֒ וְגַ֣ם אַֽחֲרֵי־כֵ֗ן אֲשֶׁ֨ר יָבֹ֜אוּ בְּנֵ֤י הָֽאֱלֹהִים֙ אֶל־בְּנ֣וֹת הָֽאָדָ֔ם וְיָלְד֖וּ לָהֶ֑ם הֵ֧מָּה הַגִּבֹּרִ֛ים אֲשֶׁ֥ר מֵעוֹלָ֖ם אַנְשֵׁ֥י הַשֵּֽׁם — "It was then, and later too, that the Nephilim appeared on earth — when divine beings cohabited with the human women, who bore them offspring. Such were the heroes [ha-gibborim] of old, the men of renown."

The phrase בְנֵי־הָֽאֱלֹהִים֙ (bene ha'elohim, "sons of God" / "the divine beings") is the same construction used elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible for members of the divine council (Job 1:6, 2:1, 38:7; Psalm 29:1, 89:6-7) — Michael Heiser's Deuteronomy 32 study lays out the philological equivalence directly, noting Ugaritic *bn 'il* ("sons of El") and *bn 'ilm* ("sons of the gods") as the linguistic and conceptual parallel to Hebrew בני אלהים, and cites Genesis 6:2, 4 alongside Job 1:6, 2:1 and Psalm 29:1, 89:6 as the standard Hebrew Bible occurrences of this exact phrase (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8 and the Sons of God," *Bibliotheca Sacra* 158 [2001], comparative table — https://www.thedivinecouncil.com/DT32BibSac.pdf).

The Nephilim (הַנְּפִלִ֞ים) and the "heroes of old, men of renown" (גִּבֹּרִ֛ים... אַֽנְשֵׁ֥י הַשֵּֽׁם) are presented as either identical to, or associated with, the offspring of these unions. This four-verse notice is famously compressed and elliptical — a fact noted by Amar Annus, who argues the Genesis author "already knew a variant of the mythology of Watchers that s/he retells in an abbreviated version," referring to the fuller narrative later developed at length in 1 Enoch 6-11 (Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," *Journal for the Study of the Pseudepigrapha* 19.4 [2010]: 316 — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240717753; archive copy https://ia600808.us.archive.org/3/items/OnTheOriginsOpfWatchersAComparativeStudyOfTheAntediluvianWisdomAnnusAmarSTAMPATO/on%20the%20origins%20opf%20watchers%20a%20comparative%20study%20of%20the%20antediluvian%20wisdom%20%20annus%20amar%20STAMPATO.pdf).

## 3. Deuteronomy 32, Especially v8-9 — The Sons of God/Sons of Israel Textual Variant

Deuteronomy 32:8-9, part of the "Song of Moses," contains one of the most significant text-critical cases bearing on divine-council theology. The Masoretic Text (MT), Sefaria's base text, reads (Sefaria, Deuteronomy 32:8-9 — https://www.sefaria.org/Deuteronomy.32.8-9):

**v.8 (MT, JPS translation):** בְּהַנְחֵ֤ל עֶלְיוֹן֙ גּוֹיִ֔ם בְּהַפְרִיד֖וֹ בְּנֵ֣י אָדָ֑ם יַצֵּב֙ גְּבֻלֹ֣ת עַמִּ֔ים לְמִסְפַּ֖ר בְּנֵ֥י יִשְׂרָאֵֽל — "When the Most High gave nations their homes / And set the divisions of humankind, / The boundaries of peoples were fixed / In relation to Israel's numbers [le-mispar bene Yisrael]."

**v.9:** כִּ֛י חֵ֥לֶק יְהֹוָ֖ה עַמּ֑וֹ יַעֲקֹ֖ב חֶ֥בֶל נַחֲלָתֽוֹ — "For the ETERNAL's portion is this people; Jacob, God's own allotment."

The MT's phrase "sons of Israel" (בני ישראל) is contested. Michael Heiser's academic article "Deuteronomy 32:8 and the Sons of God" (*Bibliotheca Sacra* 158:629 [2001]: 52-74 — full text at https://www.thedivinecouncil.com/DT32BibSac.pdf; also archived at Liberty University's DigitalCommons, https://digitalcommons.liberty.edu/lts_fac_pubs/279/) lays out the textual evidence in detail:

- The **Septuagint (LXX)**, in its predominant manuscript tradition, reads ἀγγέλων θεοῦ ("angels of God"), with several manuscripts instead reading υἱῶν θεοῦ ("sons of God") — both presupposing an underlying Hebrew of either בני אלהים or בני אלים ("sons of God/gods"), not "sons of Israel" (Heiser, p.1-2, citing Wevers, *Septuaginta: Deuteronomium*, Göttingen LXX).
- The **Dead Sea Scrolls** confirm this non-MT reading independently: "4QDt^j clearly reads בני אלוהים [bene elohim]" (Heiser, p.2, citing Sanders, *The Provenance of Deuteronomy 32*, 156, and Tov, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*, 269). A second Qumran manuscript, 4QDt^q, has "spaces for additional letters" consistent with the longer "sons of God/the gods" reading rather than "sons of Israel."
- Heiser's follow-up ETS 2010 paper states this plainly with the bracketed Qumran reading inserted directly into the translation: "Deut 32:8 When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance..., he fixed the borders of the peoples according to the number of the sons of God [בני האלהים]" and adds in a footnote: "Textual critics of the Hebrew Bible are unanimous in agreement that the Qumran reading... is superior to the Masoretic text in Deut 32:8, which reads בני ישראל ('sons of Israel')," citing P.W. Skehan's 1954 *BASOR* article publishing the Qumran fragment, Julie Duncan's Harvard dissertation on the Qumran Deuteronomy manuscripts, Emanuel Tov's *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible*, the official DJD critical edition (Ulrich et al., *Qumran Cave 4.IX*), and Jeffrey Tigay's JPS Torah Commentary on Deuteronomy (Heiser, "Should the Plural אלהים of Psalm 82 Be Understood as Men or Divine Beings?" ETS Annual Meeting 2010, p.4-5 — https://thedivinecouncil.com/ETS2010Psalm82.pdf).

Heiser's argument for why "sons of God" is original and theologically coherent, not merely text-critically probable: Ugaritic mythology "plainly states that the head of its pantheon, El (who, like the God of the Bible, is also referred to as El Elyon, the 'Most High') fathered 70 sons," matching the 70 nations of the Table of Nations in Genesis 10-11 (KTU 1.4:VI.46). This makes the "sons of God" reading numerically and conceptually coherent — the number of nations corresponds to the number of divine sons among whom Elyon apportioned them — whereas the MT's "sons of Israel" creates an unexplained anachronism (why would the number of pagan nations at Babel correspond to the size of Jacob's household, which did not yet exist)? (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.3, 23, citing Jeffrey Tigay, *JPS Torah Commentary: Deuteronomy*, 302: "Why would God base the number of nations on the number of Israelites?... verse 9... implies a contrast: Israel was God's share while the other peoples were somebody else's share, but verse 8 fails to note whose share they were.")

Combined, verses 8-9 (restoring the "sons of God" reading) depict a two-stage divine action: **Elyon** (the Most High) apportions the nations of the earth among the "sons of God" (members of the divine council), while **Yahweh's own portion** (חֵלֶק יְהֹוָה) is specifically Jacob/Israel — implying Yahweh is both identified with, and distinguished in a special covenantal role from, Elyon and the council of elohim over the other nations. Heiser further connects this reading to the divine council's textual disruption at Deuteronomy 32:43, where the DSS text (4QDeut^q) and LXX both preserve a colon referencing "sons of the divine" that is missing (evidently deliberately excised) from the MT, arguing the same theological motive — discomfort with plural divine beings — explains both variants (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.6-8, citing Tigay, *JPS Torah Commentary*, 516).

## 4. Psalm 82 — "God Stands in the Divine Assembly... He Judges Among the Gods"

Psalm 82 is the single clearest Hebrew Bible text depicting Yahweh presiding over a council of other elohim. The full Hebrew and English (JPS) text (Sefaria, Psalms 82 — https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.82):

**v.1** — מִזְמ֗וֹר לְאָ֫סָ֥ף אֱֽלֹהִ֗ים נִצָּ֥ב בַּעֲדַת־אֵ֑ל בְּקֶ֖רֶב אֱלֹהִ֣ים יִשְׁפֹּֽט — "A psalm of Asaph. God stands in the divine assembly, pronouncing judgment among the divine beings [be-qerev elohim]."

**v.6** — אֲֽנִי־אָ֭מַרְתִּי אֱלֹהִ֣ים אַתֶּ֑ם וּבְנֵ֖י עֶלְי֣וֹן כֻּלְּכֶֽם — "I had taken you for divine beings, attendants of the Most High [bene elyon], all of you."

**v.7** — אָ֭כֵן כְּאָדָ֣ם תְּמוּת֑וּן וּכְאַחַ֖ד הַשָּׂרִ֣ים תִּפֹּֽלוּ — "but you shall die as mortals do, fall like any prince."

**v.8** — קוּמָ֣ה אֱ֭לֹהִים שׇׁפְטָ֣ה הָאָ֑רֶץ כִּֽי־אַתָּ֥ה תִ֝נְחַ֗ל בְּכׇל־הַגּוֹיִֽם — "Arise, O God, judge the earth, for all the nations are Your possession."

Heiser's dedicated ETS 2010 paper on this psalm ("Should the Plural אלהים of Psalm 82 Be Understood as Men or Divine Beings?" — https://thedivinecouncil.com/ETS2010Psalm82.pdf) makes the case for the "divine beings" reading of both occurrences of אלהים in v.1 and v.6, against the traditional "human judges" reading favored by many interpreters anxious about the appearance of polytheism:

- **Grammatical argument**: "The first occurrence of אלהים is correctly translated 'God' obviously to be taken as singular for reasons of grammatical subject-verb agreement (נִצָּב). The second אלהים is equally obvious as a plural since it is the object of the preposition (בְּקֶרֶב). One cannot be in the midst of one" (Heiser, p.1).
- **Lexical range of elohim**: Heiser identifies five to six distinct classes of beings called אלהים in the Hebrew Bible — Yahweh himself, members of Yahweh's council (loyal and disloyal), the gods of foreign nations, demons (Deut 32:17), the disembodied human dead (1 Sam 28:13), and angels (Gen 35:7) — concluding that "אלהים as a term does not refer to a set of attributes or ontology"; rather, "All the figures called אלהים in the Hebrew Bible have one thing in common: they all inhabit the non-human realm. ...אלהים is what I call a 'place-of-residence' term" (Heiser, p.2-3).
- **Contextual argument from Psalm 89**: To rule out the "human judges" reading, Heiser cites Psalm 89:5-7, which places the same "sons of God" council explicitly "in the clouds" (בשחק) and "in the council (סוד) of the holy ones" — "There is no text in the entirety of the Hebrew Bible that says or suggests that there are a group of human judges in the heavens ruling with Yahweh over the nations" (Heiser, p.4).
- **Link to Deuteronomy 32**: Heiser connects Psalm 82's council directly to Deut 32:8-9 and 4:19-20 — the same elohim whom Elyon "allotted" the nations to are the ones Psalm 82 judges for corrupt administration of that allotment; the psalm's closing plea that God "inherit" (נחל, the same verb) all nations directly answers Deut 32:8's original apportionment (Heiser, p.4-5).

The 2001 *Bibliotheca Sacra* Deuteronomy 32 article (which discusses Psalm 82 in detail as its second major supporting text) further parses the difficult v.7: the phrase "you shall die like Adam [כְּאָדָ֣ם]" (not merely "like men," per Heiser's rendering) and "fall like one of the Shining Ones [הַשָּׂרִ֣ים]" parallels the fall of the divine "Shining One" (הֵילֵל) in Isaiah 14:12-15 and the divine being cast from Eden/the mountain of God in Ezekiel 28:12-17 — reinforcing that the condemned אלהים are being stripped of a divine, not human, immortality (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.10-13, notes 45-47).

Heiser also directly addresses the two classic counter-arguments: (1) that Moses is called אלהים in Exodus 4:16 and 7:1, which he argues reflects Moses functioning as a council-delegate rather than evidence אלהים routinely denotes humans; and (2) Jesus's citation of Psalm 82:6 in John 10:34-36 ("I said, you are gods"), which Heiser argues is best read as Jesus asserting membership among, and superiority to, the divine "sons of the Most High" of the psalm — not comparing himself to mere mortals (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.19-21; ETS 2010 Psalm 82 paper, §4.2).

## 5. 1 Enoch (Book of Watchers, Chapters 6-16)

The **Book of Watchers** (1 Enoch 1-36), one of the oldest sections of 1 Enoch and attested among the Qumran Aramaic fragments, gives a fully developed narrative elaborating Genesis 6:1-4. The core account (chapters 6-11) exists in two intertwined strands — the Shemihazah narrative and the Asael/Azazel narrative (Bhayro, *The Shemihazah and Asael Narrative of 1 Enoch 6-11*, AOAT 322, cited in Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.316, reference list).

**The oath and descent (ch. 6):** Two hundred angelic "Watchers" descend to Mount Hermon under the leadership of Semjaza (Shemihazah), who fears bearing the guilt alone and proposes a mutual, binding oath:

> "Semjâzâ, who was their leader, said unto them: 'I fear ye will not indeed agree to do this deed, and I alone shall have to pay the penalty of a great sin.' They all answered him and said: 'Let us all swear an oath, and all bind ourselves by mutual imprecations not to abandon this plan but to do this thing.' Then sware they all together and bound themselves by mutual imprecations upon it. They were in all two hundred; who descended in the days of Jared on the summit of Mount Hermon, and they called it Mount Hermon, because they had sworn and bound themselves by mutual imprecations upon it."
(R.H. Charles translation, 1 Enoch 6, via Wikisource, "Book of Enoch/Chapter 06" — https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Book_of_Enoch/Chapter_06)

1 Enoch 6:7 names the twenty leaders of the two hundred, including "Semjâzâ, their leader, Arâkîba, Râmêal, Kôkabîal, Tâmîal, Râmîal, Dânal, Êzêqêal, Barâqîjâl, Asâal [Azazel]..." — a list Annus notes is drawn "for the most part... from astronomical, meteorological and geographical terms, their names being related to their areas of expertise," directly paralleling the specialized functions of the Mesopotamian apkallu-sages (Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.289-90, citing Milik 1976: 29 and the *Bīt Mēseri* incantation series).

**Forbidden knowledge (ch. 7-8):** The Watchers teach humanity, and their wives specifically, a catalogue of arts. The academic Nickelsburg translation, quoted directly by Annus, gives 1 Enoch 8:3-4:

> "Shemihazah taught spells and the cutting of roots. Hermani taught sorcery for the loosing of spells and magic and skill. Baraqel taught the signs of the lightning flashes. Kokabel taught the signs of the stars. Arteqoph taught the signs of the earth. Shamsiel taught the signs of the sun. Sahriel taught the signs of the moon. And they all began to reveal mysteries to their wives and to their children. (And) as men were perishing, the cry went up to heaven."
(Nickelsburg, *1 Enoch: A Commentary*, Hermeneia [2001]: 188, quoted in Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.289-90)

Azazel (Asael) specifically is credited with metallurgy and weapon-craft, and cosmetics:

> "Asael taught men to make swords of iron and weapons and shields and breastplates and every instrument of war. He showed them metals of the earth and how they should work gold to fashion it suitably, and concerning silver, to fashion it for bracelets and ornaments for women. And he showed them concerning antimony and eye paint and all manner of precious stones and dyes."
(Nickelsburg 2001: 188, quoted in Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.288-89)

1 Enoch 10:8 summarizes: "and all the earth was made desolate by the deeds of the teaching of Asael" (Nickelsburg 2001: 215, quoted in Annus, p.289).

**Giants and violence (ch. 7):** The union of Watchers and human women produces giant offspring who devastate the earth; 1 Enoch 7:5-6 depicts them consuming human resources and finally each other, provoking the earth itself to "bring accusation against the lawless ones" — a cry that reaches heaven (Nickelsburg 2001: 182, 186, cited in Annus, p.296).

**Angelic intercession and judgment (ch. 9-11):** The four archangels — Michael, Uriel (or Sariel), Raphael, and Gabriel — "looked down from heaven and saw much blood being shed upon the earth, and all lawlessness being wrought upon the earth," and interceded before God, naming Azazel's forbidden teaching and Shemihazah's transgression specifically (1 Enoch 9:1-3, summarized with direct quotation via Reading Acts' review of the Nickelsburg/Charles text — https://readingacts.com/2016/06/02/the-archangels-render-justice-1-enoch-9-11/). God responds in chapter 10 with specific commissions (R.H. Charles translation, Wikisource "Book of Enoch/Chapter 10" — https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Book_of_Enoch/Chapter_10):

- **Uriel** is sent to warn Noah of the coming deluge so his seed may be preserved (10:1-3).
- **Raphael** is sent to "bind Azazel hand and foot, and cast him into the darkness," under rocks in the desert of Dudael, "until the day of great judgment" when he will be "cast into the fire" (10:4-8), and to heal the earth of the corruption caused by the Watchers' teaching.
- **Gabriel** is sent against the giant offspring, to set them against one another in mutual destruction (10:9-10).
- **Michael** is sent to bind Semjaza and his companions for seventy generations, until the day of judgment when they too are cast into the fiery abyss (10:11-16).

**The fate of the giants as evil spirits (ch. 15):** 1 Enoch 15:8-9 explains that the flood destroys the giants' bodies but not their spirits, which become the demons that continue to afflict humanity:

> "But now the giants who were begotten by the spirits and flesh — they will call them evil spirits of the earth, for their dwelling will be on the earth. The spirits that have gone forth from the body of their flesh are evil spirits, for from humans they came into being, and from the holy watchers was the origin of their creation. Evil spirits they will be on the earth, and evil spirits they will be called."
(Nickelsburg 2001: 267, quoted in Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.311-12)

This etiology of demons as the disembodied spirits of the antediluvian giants became foundational for later Second Temple and early Christian demonology.

## 6. Book of Giants (Dead Sea Scrolls / Qumran)

The **Book of Giants** is a distinct work of the Enochic literary tradition, attested at Qumran in Aramaic fragments (1Q23, 2Q26, 4Q203, 4Q206a, 4Q530, 4Q531, 4Q532, 4Q533, 6Q8, possibly 1Q24), and later transmitted independently within Manichaean literature in Middle Persian, Sogdian, and other languages. Its critical edition and translation is by Loren T. Stuckenbruck, *The Book of Giants from Qumran: Texts, Translation, and Commentary* (Tübingen: J.C.B. Mohr, 1997), and the DJD critical edition of 4Q530 is by Émile Puech, "4Q530. 4QLivre des Géants^b ar," in *Qumrân Grotte 4 XXII: Textes araméens* (DJD 31; Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2001), 27-43.

**Content and relationship to 1 Enoch:** The Book of Giants narrates events from the perspective of the giants themselves — the offspring of the Watchers and human women (as in 1 Enoch 6-16) — including prophetic dream-visions of the coming destruction. Amar Annus summarizes: "4Q530 2 ii describes two visions, one disclosed to the giant Hahyah and the other to his brother Ohyah. Hahyah has a dream of a large garden tended by gardeners, and fire and water destroy the grove, except for one tree with three branches. Ohyah has a dream in which he sees God descend and sit on a throne, with a heavenly host in attendance" (search-result summary corroborated in Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.293, discussing 4Q530 2 i-ii and citing Puech 2001: 28-38).

Annus quotes the fragment directly: "the gardeners were watering" (*gnnyn hww mšqyn*, 4Q530 2 ii 7) — read as an allusion to the Watchers' original, licit educational mission before their corruption — and "[the] water, and fire burned it all" (*kl my' wnwr' dlq bkl*, 4Q530 2 i 10), describing the destruction of the garden/vegetation, symbolizing the coming flood and the giants' destruction (Annus, p.293, 309, citing Puech 2001: 28, 33-34).

**Gilgamesh and Humbaba as giants:** The single most striking feature of the Book of Giants is that it names two of the giants **Gilgamesh** and **Humbaba** — borrowing directly from the Mesopotamian Gilgamesh epic tradition. Annus states: "The names of Gilgamesh, Humbaba and Uta-napishti occur in different recensions of the Book of Giants as names of the gigantic offspring of the Watchers. According to J. Reeves, 'this represents a bold polemical thrust against the revered traditions of a rival culture' (Reeves 1992: 126). L.T. Stuckenbruck has argued that knowledge of the Epic of Gilgamesh 'was not restricted to the mere use of names derived from it but is reflected in the broad narrative of BG [Book of Giants] itself' (2003: 333)" (Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.281-82, citing John C. Reeves, *Jewish Lore in Manichaean Cosmology: Studies in the Book of Giants* [Cincinnati: Hebrew Union College Press, 1992], and Stuckenbruck, "Giant Mythology and Demonology," in *Die Dämonen — Demons* [Tübingen: Mohr Siebeck, 2003]: 318-38). Search results corroborate that "Ohyah speaks to Gilgamesh regarding a dream he had" within the surviving fragments, connecting the giant protagonists to the Gilgamesh figure directly within the narrative (per summary drawing on Stuckenbruck's edition).

Independent cuneiform evidence that Gilgamesh himself was remembered as gigantic — "eleven cubits was his height" — is confirmed by a newly published Ugarit fragment of the Gilgamesh epic (George, "The Gilgameš Epic at Ugarit," *AuOr* 25 [2007]: 240, l.34, cited in Annus, p.283), which independently corroborates why Second Temple Jewish authors could plausibly cast Gilgamesh as a Nephilim-type giant.

**Relation to 1 Enoch:** The Book of Giants functions as a companion/sequel text within the same Enochic tradition as the Book of Watchers, expanding on the fate of the giant offspring named but not detailed at length in 1 Enoch 7 and 15. Enoch himself appears in the Manichaean recensions of the text as "the apostle" who is sent to interpret the giants' dream-visions of doom (Annus, p.304, citing Milik 1976: 313 and Stuckenbruck 1997: 75-76, 85-86).

## 7. Mesopotamian Divine Council Texts — Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, and the *Puḫru*

Mesopotamian religion had a well-developed concept of a divine assembly (Akkadian *puḫru*; the term for the specific convened body being *puḫur ilāni*, "assembly of the gods"), structured hierarchically with senior gods (frequently termed **Anunnaki**) presiding over junior/laboring gods (the **Igigi**).

### Enuma Elish (Babylonian Epic of Creation)

The *Enuma Elish* narrates the primordial conflict between the younger gods and the sea-goddess Tiamat, resolved through the elevation of Marduk to kingship by decree of the divine assembly.

**Tiamat's rebellion:** After the death of Apsu, Tiamat — enraged and counseled by Qingu — musters an army: she "created the Hydra, the Dragon, the Hairy Hero / The Great Demon, the Savage Dog, and the Scorpion-man" among eleven monstrous beings (Tablet I), and "gave him [Qingu] the Tablet of Destinies and fastened it to his breast," making him supreme commander of her forces (Enuma Elish Tablet I, lines 141-142, 157; text via World History Encyclopedia's full translation — https://www.worldhistory.org/article/225/enuma-elish---the-babylonian-epic-of-creation---fu/).

**The gods' fear and the assembly's paralysis:** Facing Tiamat, the senior gods Ea and Anu each in turn approach and retreat in fear; Ea reports: "Her strength is mighty, she is full of dread, / She is altogether very strong, none can go against her" (Tablet II, lines 87-88, World History Encyclopedia translation).

**Marduk's condition — kingship in exchange for combat:** Marduk agrees to fight Tiamat only if the assembled gods grant him supreme, irrevocable authority beforehand: "If I should become your avenger, / If I should bind Tiamat and preserve you, / Convene an assembly and proclaim for me an exalted destiny... let me, with my utterance, decree destinies instead of you" (Tablet II, lines 156-160, World History Encyclopedia translation). Scholar Wayne Horowitz, writing for TheTorah.com, summarizes the same episode: "If I am to defeat Tiamat and save your lives, / Convene the council, name a special fate" (Horowitz, "Enuma Elish: Babylonia's Creation Myth and the Enthronement of Marduk," TheTorah.com — https://www.thetorah.com/article/enuma-elish-babylonias-creation-myth-and-the-enthronement-of-marduk).

**The assembly's proclamation of kingship:** After Marduk's victory, the convened gods (in the assembly hall Ubshukinna) formally elevate him: "O Marduk, you are honored among the great gods, / Your destiny is unequalled, your word (has the power of) Anu!" (Tablet IV, quoted by Horowitz, TheTorah.com). Tablet VII closes the epic with the assembly conferring on Marduk fifty divine names summarizing the powers and portfolios of the other gods, consolidated in him: "By the name of 'Fifty' did the great gods / Proclaim his fifty names, they made his path pre-eminent" (Tablet VII, per search-corroborated translation of the standard text).

Horowitz's academic analysis situates this text as the liturgical centerpiece of the Babylonian New Year (Akitu) festival, annually re-enacting and legitimating both cosmic order and the king's earthly rule by analogy to Marduk's assembly-granted kingship — and argues this "imagery of divine kingship from the yearly enthroning of Marduk" was directly available to the Judean exiles in Babylon and shaped later biblical/liturgical depictions of Yahweh's kingship (Horowitz, TheTorah.com, cited above).

### Atrahasis

The Old Babylonian *Atrahasis* epic opens with the two-tier divine hierarchy — greater gods (**Anunnaki**) versus laboring gods (**Igigi**) — and the flood as the assembly's judicial response to human overpopulation and noise. A composite ANET-based translation (Lambert & Millard) summarized via Livius.org states the pantheon "is subdivided into two groups: the Anunnaki and the Igigi — the greater and the lesser gods," with "the Seven great Anunnaki... making the Igigu suffer the work" of digging canals and irrigation before humanity's creation (Livius.org, "The Epic of Atraḫasis," ANET translation — https://www.livius.org/sources/content/anet/104-106-the-epic-of-atrahasis/).

**The Igigi rebellion:** After forty years of forced labor, the Igigi revolt: "Let us face up to our foreman the prefect, / he must take off our heavy burden upon us!" (lines 40-41); they "set fire to their tools" and marched on "the gate of the warrior Enlil's abode" (lines 64-68) — a direct labor strike against the divine hierarchy (Livius.org, ANET translation of Atrahasis, Tablet I).

**The assembly's decision to create humankind:** Facing the Igigi's revolt, the divine assembly — described in the text as the "Lower Gods" declaring "excessive drudgery has killed us, our forced labor was heavy" (lines 149-150) — decides collectively that humans should be created specifically to "assume the drudgery of the god" (line 197), delegating the Igigi's burden to a newly created servant class (Livius.org, ANET translation of Atrahasis).

**The flood decision:** Later in the epic, humanity's population growth and "noise" (*rigmu*) disturb the gods' rest, and the divine assembly — led by Enlil — resolves to destroy humankind via successive plagues, drought, and finally deluge; Enki/Ea, dissenting, secretly warns the flood-hero Atrahasis in a dream: "Flee the house, build a boat" (Livius.org, ANET translation of Atrahasis, Tablet III). The parallel between this ***divine council decreeing the flood*** and Genesis's flood account (and its Watchers-narrative elaboration in 1 Enoch) has long been a touchstone of comparative ANE scholarship.

### The Concept of *Puḫru*

Across Sumerian and Akkadian literature more broadly, the *puḫru* (assembly/council) functions as the structural mechanism of divine governance: major decisions — creation, kingship, war, judicial punishment of humanity — are enacted not by unilateral divine fiat but by convened deliberation and proclamation among an assembly of gods headed by a chief deity (El at Ugarit, Anu/Enlil/Marduk at various periods in Mesopotamia). Michael Heiser's Deuteronomy 32 study situates the biblical "divine council" concept as a cognate Northwest Semitic/Mesopotamian institution, citing E. Theodore Mullen's foundational *The Divine Council in Canaanite and Early Hebrew Literature* (Harvard Semitic Monographs 24, 1980) as "the major work on the divine council," alongside Lowell K. Handy's *Among the Host of Heaven: The Syro-Palestinian Pantheon as Bureaucracy* (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.8, n.34).

## 8. The Apkallu — Antediluvian Sages and Their Comparison to the Watchers

The **apkallu** (Akkadian; Sumerian *abgal*) are the seven legendary antediluvian sages of Mesopotamian tradition, credited with bringing the arts of civilization to humankind before the flood. The definitive modern academic study connecting them to the Enochic Watchers tradition is Amar Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers: A Comparative Study of the Antediluvian Wisdom in Mesopotamian and Jewish Traditions," *Journal for the Study of the Pseudepigrapha* 19.4 (2010): 277-320 (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240717753; PDF: https://ia600808.us.archive.org/3/items/OnTheOriginsOpfWatchersAComparativeStudyOfTheAntediluvianWisdomAnnusAmarSTAMPATO/on%20the%20origins%20opf%20watchers%20a%20comparative%20study%20of%20the%20antediluvian%20wisdom%20%20annus%20amar%20STAMPATO.pdf).

**Abstract/thesis:** "In the article, it is argued that the origin of Watchers derives from the Mesopotamian mythology of the antediluvian sages (*apkallus*). More precisely, it is proposed that the mythology of Watchers and their sons the giants derived from inverted versions of various Mesopotamian myths and beliefs about *apkallus*" (Annus, p.277, Abstract).

**Oannes and the transmission of civilization:** The earliest and most complete surviving account of the apkallu tradition comes from Berossus, a third-century BCE Babylonian priest writing in Greek, describing the first sage Oannes:

> "In the first year a beast named Oannes appeared from the Erythrean Sea in a place adjacent to Babylonia. Its entire body was that of a fish, but a human head had grown beneath the head of the fish and human feet likewise had grown from the fish's tail. It also had a human voice... It gave to the men the knowledge of letters and sciences and crafts of all types. It also taught them how to found cities, establish temples, introduce laws and measure land. It also revealed to them seeds and the gathering of fruits, and in general it gave men everything which is connected with the civilized life."
(Berossus, Fragment 1, translated in Burstein, *The Babyloniaca of Berossus* [Malibu: Undena, 1978]: 13-14, quoted in Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.283)

Later sages, per the *Bīt Mēseri* incantation series, include figures such as **Adapa** (also called Uanadapa/U-An, the likely source of Berossus's "Oannes"), whom Annus identifies with the seventh position in the sage-list, paralleling the biblical seventh antediluvian patriarch Enoch — a connection scholars trace to the Mesopotamian king **Enmeduranki**, the seventh antediluvian king in the Sumerian King List, who "distinguished himself with divine knowledge from the gods Adad and Shamash" (Annus, p.278, quoting John J. Collins, *The Apocalyptic Imagination*: 26, 45-46: "The figure of Enoch is to some degree modelled on Enmeduranki... Evidently the biblical seventh man emulates the Mesopotamian seventh king").

**The comparison to the Watchers — Annus's core argument:** Annus identifies several direct points of structural correspondence:

1. **Divine status and mating with humans:** "The 'sons of God' in Genesis and the Watchers in Enochic literature are fully divine, as also were the antediluvian *apkallus* in the Mesopotamian tradition. The four post-flood *apkallus* were 'of human descent,' which means that *apkallus* could mate with humans, as the Watchers did" (Annus, p.281-82, citing A.D. Kilmer 1987: 39-40).
2. **Forbidden/dangerous knowledge:** The list of arts the Watchers teach in 1 Enoch 8:3-4 (spellcraft, root-cutting, sorcery, celestial omens, metallurgy) directly mirrors the corpus of Mesopotamian priestly sciences — exorcism (*āšipūtu*), astrology (*tupšarrūtu*), omen interpretation (*bārûtu*) — all of which Mesopotamian colophons explicitly attribute to transmission "from the mouth of Ea" through the antediluvian sages, and which were guarded as secret, restricted knowledge ("secrets of the antediluvian sages," *niṣirti apkallī*) not to be taught to the unqualified (Annus, p.286-91, citing the "Catalogue of Texts and Authors" and colophon formulae).
3. **The apkallu's own "dark side" in Mesopotamian tradition:** Annus's most original contribution is demonstrating the demonization of the Watchers was not purely a Jewish innovation: apkallu-sages already appear as malevolent witches in the Mesopotamian anti-witchcraft series *Maqlû* — "My warlocks are the Sages of the Apsû, my witches are the heavenly Daughters of Anu" (*Maqlû* III 61-76, quoted in Annus, p.300) — and individual sages are recorded as having angered the gods through hubris (e.g., Adapa breaking the South Wind's wing in the Adapa myth) (Annus, p.281, 296-97).
4. **Judgment by flood:** Just as the Watchers and giants are judged and imprisoned/destroyed by the deluge in 1 Enoch 10, the Erra Epic depicts Marduk banishing the apkallu-sages to the Apsû at the time of the flood: "I sent craftsmen down to Apsû, I ordered them not to come up" (Erra I 147, quoted in Annus, p.309).
5. **The names Gilgamesh, Humbaba, and Uta-napishti as giants** in the Book of Giants (see §6 above) form, in Annus's reading, part of the same deliberate polemical inversion: figures of Mesopotamian wisdom and heroism are recast as monstrous, judged antediluvian beings in the Jewish retelling (Annus, p.281-83).

**Conclusion of the study:** "The mythology of the Watchers and their sons the giants derived from inverted versions of various Mesopotamian myths and beliefs about *apkallus*. In some parts or layers of Mesopotamian mythology and ritual practices the sages were already regarded as dangerous and potentially malicious creatures, upon which the Jewish authors could build their parody" (Annus, p.315, Conclusion). Annus further speculates the *Book of Watchers* may function as coded polemic against a "Babylonianized" Jerusalem priesthood in the early Second Temple period, given its structural parallel to the also-Babylon-styled portrayal of Daniel as a wise courtier (Annus, p.315-16).

## Synthesis: Shared Motifs

Read together, these eight bodies of text reveal a coherent, historically-connected constellation of ideas spanning roughly two thousand years of ancient Near Eastern religious literature, from third-millennium Sumerian sage-lists through Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic. Several motifs recur with enough specificity — shared vocabulary, shared narrative structure, shared numerology — that mainstream biblical scholarship treats them not as coincidental parallels but as evidence of direct cultural and literary contact and inheritance.

**1. The heavenly/divine council as a common ANE governing concept.** Every culture surveyed here — Sumerian, Akkadian/Babylonian, Ugaritic/Canaanite, and Israelite — organizes its heavenly order as a convened assembly (Akkadian *puḫru*; Ugaritic *ʿdt ʾilm*/Hebrew עֲדַת אֵל, Ps 82:1; Hebrew סוֹד, "council," Jer 23:18, 22) presided over by a chief deity (El at Ugarit, Anu/Enlil/Marduk in Mesopotamia, Yahweh in Israel), who deliberates, judges, and commissions messengers through it (1 Kings 22:19-23; Isaiah 6; the *Enuma Elish* assembly at Ubshukinna; the Atrahasis assembly deciding humanity's creation and destruction). Heiser's comparative philological table in the Deuteronomy 32 study documents near-exact terminological correspondences between Ugaritic and biblical Hebrew council vocabulary — *ʿdt ʾilm* / עֲדַת אֵל, *ph̬r kkbm* ("congregation of the stars") / כּוֹכְבֵי בֹקֶר ("morning stars," Job 38:7), *ph̬r mʿd* / הַר מוֹעֵד ("mount of assembly," Isa 14:13) — arguing this reflects genuine, traceable linguistic and religious inheritance rather than independent, coincidental invention (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.14-18).

**2. "Sons of God"/*elohim* as a distinct class of divine beings.** Genesis 6:2,4's *bene ha'elohim*, Deuteronomy 32:8's *bene elohim* (restored per LXX/DSS), Psalm 82:1,6's *elohim*/*bene elyon*, and Job 1:6/2:1/38:7's *bene elohim* all denote members of a divine class subordinate to, but ontologically continuous with, the chief god — the same category as Ugaritic *bn ʾil*/*bn ʾilm* ("sons of El"/"sons of the gods") and, on Annus's argument, structurally equivalent to the fully-divine Mesopotamian apkallu-sages. Heiser's central technical claim — that Hebrew אלהים is "a place-of-residence term" denoting any non-human, non-corporeal inhabitant of the divine realm rather than a fixed set of unique attributes — is offered specifically to explain how the Hebrew Bible can affirm a plurality of elohim (Gen 3:22; Ps 82:1,6; Ps 89:6-7; Deut 32:8, 43) while still asserting Yahweh's unrivaled supremacy within that class (Heiser, ETS 2010 Psalm 82 paper, §2.2, §4.2.6 "The Divine Council and Israelite Monotheism").

**3. Rebellion or transgression among divine beings.** Genesis 6's sons of God overstepping their bounds; the Watchers' oath-bound conspiracy on Hermon (1 Enoch 6); Tiamat's insurrection and the Igigi's labor strike in *Enuma Elish* and *Atrahasis*; the apkallu Adapa's hubris against the South Wind; and Psalm 82's elohim judged and stripped of immortality "for their corrupt administration" — all instantiate a shared ANE narrative pattern in which subordinate divine beings transgress an allotted boundary and are judged for it by the chief deity or assembly.

**4. Illicit transmission of forbidden knowledge.** The single tightest point of correspondence Annus documents: the Watchers' catalogue of taught arts (metallurgy, cosmetics, root-cutting, celestial/meteorological omens, sorcery — 1 Enoch 8:1-4) maps almost item-for-item onto the corpus of Mesopotamian priestly sciences attributed to the apkallu and to Ea "from his mouth" — except that in the Babylonian tradition this transmission is *lawful and civilization-founding* (Oannes/Berossus), while in 1 Enoch it is recast as *illegitimate and catastrophic*, a "hilarious irony" and "polemical stance" Annus argues 2nd-Temple Jewish scribes deliberately constructed against their Mesopotamian scholarly counterparts (Annus, "On the Origin of Watchers," p.291).

**5. Giants/hybrid offspring.** The Nephilim/gibborim of Genesis 6:4; the Watchers' giant sons in 1 Enoch 7 and 15, whose slain bodies release disembodied evil-spirit "demons"; and the Book of Giants' cast of named giants borrowed directly from Mesopotamian epic (Gilgamesh, Humbaba, Uta-napishti) — all represent the same mythological category of dangerous hybrid beings born from the boundary-transgression of category (2)/(3) above, and in the Jewish material specifically become the etiological origin of demons.

**6. Mainstream biblical scholarship and the "evolution" of Israelite monotheism.** A distinct scholarly lineage — E. Theodore Mullen (*The Divine Council in Canaanite and Early Hebrew Literature*, 1980), Frank Moore Cross (*Canaanite Myth and Hebrew Epic*, 1973; "The Council of Yahweh in Second Isaiah," *JNES* 12 [1953]), and Michael Heiser building on both — reads Deuteronomy 32:8-9 and Psalm 82 as textual fossils documenting a stage of Israelite religion in which Yahweh was understood as one *elohim* among a populated pantheon (functioning, in the earliest layer, as a distinct figure from El Elyon, who allots the nations, before the two are identified) rather than as the sole existent deity. On this reading, Deuteronomy 32:8-9's original "sons of God" text (preserved at Qumran and in the LXX, suppressed in the later Masoretic tradition) is a direct textual witness to a Yahweh-among-many-elohim theological stage that later scribal tradition found necessary to obscure — the same impulse Heiser identifies in the *tiqqune sopherim* ("corrections of the scribes") and in the parallel excision at Deut 32:43 (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.6-8, 23-27). Heiser is explicit, however, that he reads this data as compatible with — not disproof of — a coherent Israelite monotheism properly defined: his argument is that the Hebrew Bible's authors held Yahweh to be *unique in kind* (uncreated, sole creator, unrivaled) even while affirming the real existence of a populated, hierarchical council of lesser, created *elohim* — a position he distinguishes from the classic history-of-religions thesis (associated with earlier scholarship reaching back through Cross to Julius Wellhausen-era source criticism) that Israelite religion is best described as having "evolved" in a linear, unidirectional sense from polytheism to monotheism. Heiser's own framing states the tension directly: "This author contends that the choice of MT in Deuteronomy 32:8 is based on a misunderstanding of both the textual history of the Hebrew Bible and text-critical methodology... The primary goal of the present article is to show that understanding 'sons of God' as the correct reading in Deuteronomy 32:8 in no way requires one to view Israelite religion as polytheistic" (Heiser, "Deuteronomy 32:8," p.3). Whether one follows Heiser's own theological reconciliation or the more straightforwardly evolutionary reading associated with Mullen and Cross, the underlying philological and text-critical facts they all draw on — the LXX/Qumran "sons of God" variant at Deut 32:8, the plural *elohim* of Ps 82:1,6, and the Ugaritic comparative material establishing the linguistic pedigree of this language — are treated across this entire scholarly spectrum as solid documentary evidence that Israel's religious vocabulary and cosmology emerged out of, and initially shared substantial structural continuity with, the broader West Semitic/Mesopotamian divine-council worldview described throughout this document, before later scribal and theological developments moved decisively toward the strict, exclusive monotheism of post-exilic and rabbinic Judaism.
